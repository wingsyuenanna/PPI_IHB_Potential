# Widen three CAPEX bands before rerunning

For the next EU facility LCOH run, treat the current heat-pump pair as a **low, equipment-lean case** and test Danish installed costs as the central and high cases; vary solid-media heat-battery **storage** inside a media-and-containment band; and leave the 2030 lithium-ion point where it is, near the middle of a learning band. State heat-pump sensitivities in **2025 euros** (the Danish catalogue’s price level): about **400 / 900 / 1,200–1,500 € per kW_th** below 100 °C, and **800 / 1,550 / 1,950 € per kW_th** for 100–150 °C hot water, the same hot-water central case with steam, and steam plus a large temperature lift. The model’s CPI-inflated 2030 costs, **$918 and $1,141 per kW_th**, match the low equipment end of that scale and do not match an installed European project. Solid-media storage, in **mixed-year nominal USD (about 2018–2025)**, is **$2–5 / $5–15 / $15–40 per kWh**, which puts the model’s **$5.83/kWh (2030 USD)** at the **bottom of the central media band**; charge power only supports a scope span from about **$21/kW** of resistive heaters to the model’s own **$100/kW (2023 USD)**, and delivery should stay at **$300/kW (2023 USD)** because no independent heat-exchanger range was found. A vendor total-installed figure of **$85–210/kWh** is an alternative whole-battery price, not a high storage term to add on top of separate charge and delivery power. Four-hour lithium-ion for a multi-MW EU site is **$150 / $230 / $330 per kWh in real 2025 USD** and **$120 / $180 / $280 per kWh in real ~2030 USD**, so the model’s **$253/kWh in 2025** is slightly high and **$182/kWh in 2030** is the central learning case. Pack prices and molten-salt CSP storage are different products and stay out of these rows. No source in the notes was converted onto the model’s 2030-USD basis, so the bands below keep each source’s own currency year.

Two coding rules sit on top of the table. First, **do not add a bundled $/kWh to the three-part heat battery**. Company total installed cost is about **$85–210/kWh for installation by 2025** and **$40–110/kWh by 2030**, with one 2030 modeling point at **$57.5/kWh**, and that bundle already includes manufacturing and installation ([Brattle, Thermal Batteries, 2023](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Thermal-Batteries-Opportunities-to-Accelerate-Decarbonization-of-Industrial-Heat.pdf)). Using it as “high storage” while the model still adds charge and delivery **$/kW** double-counts power equipment. If a scenario uses the bundle, drop the separate **$100/kW** and **$300/kW** lines. Second, **do not CPI-inflate the 2025 lithium-ion cost to make a 2030 case**. The model’s move from about **$253/kWh to $182/kWh** is already a learning decline of about 28%, and every 2030 forecast in the notes is a real-cost path that falls or flattens. A CPI-only path would move the wrong direction.

For 2030 heat pumps, the same Danish table supports holding the 2025 central case and applying only about **−4% to −7%**: hot water to 150 °C goes from **1.55 to 1.50 M€/MW**, and steam to 125 °C from **1.40 to 1.30 M€/MW**, still in **2025 euros** ([Danish Energy Agency process-heat catalogue, January 2026](https://ens.dk/media/7757/download)). That is a catalogue judgement, not a contracted 2030 price, and it is a decline. The model’s heat-pump 2030 figures are the opposite operation: 2021 costs marked up with CPI. Do not stack the older catalogue’s roughly 10–11% decline from 2020 to 2030 on top of the 2026 table.

## Danish installed heat pumps price the Rissman pair as low

The model’s **$700 and $870 per kW_th (2021 USD)**, and the CPI-inflated **$918 and $1,141 (2030 USD)**, come from one equipment-lean source (Rissman 2022 on an Agora basis). They line up with US economic-to-technical mechanical vapour-compression costs of **$400 and $800 per kW of heat** and with a vapour-compression installed summary of **$250–800 per kW**, dollar year unlabeled, report dated March 2022 ([ACEEE ie2201](https://www.aceee.org/sites/default/files/pdfs/ie2201.pdf)). They also line up with an older Danish traditional-unit cross-reference of **0.73 M€/MW** and with 2020 hybrid absorption/compression costs of **0.87 M€/MW** up to 125 °C and **1.05 M€/MW** up to 150 °C, in a catalogue whose financial section is fixed at the 2019 price level ([earlier DEA process-heat PDF](https://ens.dk/media/3307/download)). They do not line up with the January 2026 Danish installed table.

That 2026 table is the right central case when the heat-pump CAPEX line is the only capital line for the project. Financial figures are **real 2025 euros, excluding VAT**. Nominal investment is an overnight, EPC-style cost: equipment plus installation (engineering, civil works, buildings, on-site grid connection, commissioning). It includes the heat pump, piping to an **existing** hot-water or steam loop, and minimal construction. It excludes VAT, owners’ development, interest during construction, lost production, dismantling, a new off-plot supply system, an undersized electrical connection, and the cost of converting a steam circuit to hot water. In **2025**, hot water is **1.20 M€/MW** up to 125 °C and **1.55 M€/MW** up to 150 °C; steam is **1.40 and 1.75 M€/MW**. In **2030** those four figures are **1.15, 1.50, 1.30, and 1.65**. A worked 2 MW example, source 60 °C and sink 140 °C steam (80 K lift), starts at 1.55, adds **0.20 M€/MW** for steam equipment and **0.20 M€/MW** for lift above 75 K, and lands at **1.95 M€/MW** ([DEA 2026 PDF](https://ens.dk/media/7757/download)). The catalogue also says a cascade is almost twice a single-stage system in **equipment** cost. The jump from the 2019/2020 hybrid table (0.87–1.05 M€/MW) to the 2026 hot-water table (1.20–1.55 M€/MW) is a re-estimate of scope and quotes, not a price-index update. The 2026 Excel for a traditional unit below 100 °C was not in the narrative extract, so the **900 €/kW_th** central case below 100 °C is the high end of that older evidence, not a 2026 datasheet cell.

The low case is supplier equipment **without integration**. IEA HPT Annex 58, from a 2022–2023 supplier survey that was not third-party validated, reports **200–1,500 €/kW** for an installed system without integration, and **200–1,200 €/kW** when installation and integration are disregarded; many commercial compression machines sit inside **200–800 €/kW** ([Annex 58 Task 1](https://heatpumpingtechnologies.org/annex58/wp-content/uploads/sites/70/2023/09/annex-58-task-1-technologies-task-report.pdf)). Named bands in that survey and the related slides include Hybrid Energy **200–600 €/kW** (to about 120 °C, without integration), Siemens Energy **250–800**, Heaten about **250–450**, and Olvondo **1,200** ([Hybrid Energy sheet](https://heatpumpingtechnologies.org/content/uploads/sites/70/2022/07/annex58-hybrid-energy-juli-2022-rev3.pdf)). Swiss 2024 quotes for a ~550 kW design, sink about 115 °C, excluding planning and integration, are **840–1,000 EUR/kW** for HFO machines and **1,300 EUR/kW** for n-butane, and the same slides apply a **planning and integration factor of 2.0** ([Spiess, 7 Nov 2024](https://www.sweet-decarb.ch/fileadmin/downloads/Presentations_File/2024_11_07_HTHP_webinar_Gustav_Spiess_Case_Study.pdf)). Apply that factor to an equipment quote, not to the Danish nominal investment, which already includes installation. Above 150 °C, a 2019 bottom-up total capital investment was about **950 €/kW** at ~50 MW and about **1,870–2,000 €/kW** at ~8 MW ([Zühlsdorf et al. 2019](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/195360755/Analysis_of_technologies_and_potentials_for_heat_pump_based.pdf)). NREL’s US defaults, dollar year unlabeled beyond an August 2023 report date, are **$300/kW_th** in a 120 °C default case and **$800/kW_th** in a beer-brewing steam case at 115 °C, with an explicit warning not to use them as standards ([NREL/TP-7A40-84560](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy23osti/84560.pdf)).

At any 2020s rate near **1.0–1.2 USD per EUR**, **1,550 €/kW** remains well above **$870/kW**. The gap is scope and vintage. No FX or CPI chain was applied in the notes, so do not replace **$918 / $1,141** with a converted Danish figure until that chain is explicit. JRC’s **EUR 250–2,000 per installed kW** is a building-heat-pump statement and is not an industrial row ([JRC144191](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC144191/JRC144191_01.pdf)). A secondary annex that lists **500 / 700 / 870 / 1,300 EUR/kW_th** by temperature band has no currency year, and the 870 matches the Rissman 2021 USD figure with no stated conversion, so it is not a second measurement ([Reform Institute annex](https://ireform.eu/s/uploads/Clean_industrial_heat_Electrification_scenarios_for_selected_sectors_ANNEX.pdf)).

Fixed O&M stays paired with the CAPEX it came from. NREL’s default next to **$300/kW_th** is **$11.8 per kW_th-year** plus **$0.05/kWh_th** variable. ACEEE uses **3% of CapEx per year** for mechanical vapour compression, which is about **$12/kW-year** on a $400/kW machine and about **$24/kW-year** on an $800/kW machine. The model’s **$12 and $18 per kW_th-year (2021 USD)** belong with **$700 and $870**. The 2026 Danish narrative does not fill the fixed-O&M cell. Scaling ACEEE’s 3% onto a **1,400–1,600 €/kW** machine implies roughly **40–50 € per kW_th-year**, which is a different maintenance intensity. Do not apply 3% unless that definition is accepted. If the sink is steam rather than hot water, add **200 €/kW**; if the lift exceeds 75 K, add another **200 €/kW**. Those two adders are what take **1,550 €/kW** to **1,950 €/kW**.

## Vary heat-battery storage, and do not stack a vendor total

The model’s three-part build is Energy Innovation, July 2023: **$100/kW** electrical input, **$300/kW** heat-delivery equipment, **$5/kWh** storage (stated band **$1.5–$10/kWh**), round-trip **95%**, and no separate O&M line in the cost buildup that was read ([Industrial Thermal Batteries](https://energyinnovation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/2023-07-13-Industrial-Thermal-Batteries-Report-v133.pdf)). CPI inflation to about **$117/kW, $350/kW, and $5.83/kWh** in 2030 USD does not create a new source. The **$1.5–$10/kWh** band cites an NREL particle electricity-storage concept and a 2023 Antora Q&A, so the CSV low and high are not an independent industrial-heat catalogue. Independent component studies put simple solid stores (brick, rock, sand), with power equipment excluded or minimal, at a few dollars per kWh, and put realized high-temperature regenerators and vendor modules in the tens of dollars per kWh.

Use these storage anchors, and only these, for the low, central, and high media cases. A 2025 Applied Energy model of firebrick resistance-heated storage reports **$4.55/kWhth**, **$20.67/kW** for additional resistive heaters, and **$6.47/kW** for pumps and blowers; currency year is unstated, charge efficiency is 0.99, discharge to air is 0.85, and the authors assume O&M at **2.5% of amortized CAPEX** over a 30-year NPV life ([McMillan et al., Applied Energy 2025](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)). A Danish rock-bed project heated to 600 °C estimates hot rocks and capsule, excluding the heat-recovery steam generator, at **2.3 €/kWh** for 2,500 MWh ([EUDP HT-TES final report](https://eudp.dk/files/media/document/Final%20report%20HT%20TES%20-%20the%20end.pdf)). A US DOE electricity-storage pathway says silica sand “has the potential” for installed costs near **$2/kWht**; that is a potential, not a 2025 EPC price ([DOE Storage Innovations 2030](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf)). The high case is a different object: IEA ECES Annex 30 reports a realized steelmaking regenerator at **15–40 €/kWh** (1,250–1,400 °C, 140 MWh and 140 MW, so short-duration power equipment is inside the energy cost; euro year unstated) ([Annex 30, September 2018](https://iea-es.org/wp-content/uploads/public/Applications-of-Thermal-Energy-Storag-in-the-Energy-Transition-Annex-30-Report-1.pdf)); IEA’s industry summary says sand, cement, and bricks up to 1,000 °C cost “around **USD 15–20 per kWh**,” without stating year, duration, or whether heaters and heat exchangers are included ([IEA, Renewables for Industry](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-for-industry/executive-summary)); EnergyNest states HEATCRETE at “around **20–25 US dollars per kilowatt hours**,” year unstated ([EnergyNest](https://energy-nest.com/industry_application/)). IRENA’s industry sensible band of **0.1–35 USD/kWh (2018)** spans about −150 °C to 1,000 °C and mixes in tank storage, so it is not a brick-battery quote ([IRENA TES outlook 2020](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2020/Nov/IRENA_Innovation_Outlook_TES_2020.pdf)).

On that evidence the model’s **$5.83/kWh** is a fair **low-central media** cost, next to the Stack figure of **$4.55/kWhth**, and it is not a high case. The charge term is the awkward one. The only non-baseline component number is about **$21/kW** for heaters. The model’s **$100/kW (2023 USD)** is the next published point, and it buys electrical balance of plant rather than a second heater quote. A span of roughly **$20–$100/kW** is therefore a scope span. No 2030 learning rate for that term was found, so do not mark the CPI-inflated **$117/kW** down. Delivery is thinner still. **$6.47/kW** is hot-air blowers and is the wrong scope for steam or a process heat exchanger. No independent low, central, and high for a heat-exchanger or steam-delivery skid was found, so leave delivery at **$300/kW (2023 USD)** and vary storage energy only. The model’s zero OPEX is a choice; the only process-heat O&M figure retrieved is the Applied Energy assumption of 2.5% of amortized CAPEX. Round-trip **95%** sits inside Brattle’s **90–98%** when the output is heat ([Brattle 2023](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Thermal-Batteries-Opportunities-to-Accelerate-Decarbonization-of-Industrial-Heat.pdf)). Do not replace it with the EUDP air-pilot round trip of about **78%**, or with a molten-salt-plus-steam-turbine electricity round trip of **44%**.

Vendor totals that look like one number per kWh are not storage-medium prices. Antora’s own commission report says thermal batteries could be deployed “for less than **$10 per kWh**” in a paragraph about long-duration electricity storage with thermophotovoltaics ([CEC-500-2024-011](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2024-02/CEC-500-2024-011.pdf)), while a grant listing anticipates installed capital “**<$0.05/kWh**” ([Energize Innovation](https://www.energizeinnovation.fund/projects/solid-state-long-duration-energy-storage-industrial-applications)). Those two vendor projections conflict if both are read as installed capital, and neither is a central 2025 CAPEX. Rondo’s “well within **$20 a megawatt-hour**” and Lumenion’s **€0.02 per kWh** are levelized costs of stored energy, not capacity CAPEX. No public invoice was found for Rondo, Antora, Kraftblock, Electrified Thermal Solutions, Brenmiller, or Kyoto.

Molten-salt CSP storage is a separate product and a separate sensitivity. Commercial nitrate two-tank systems in 2018–2022 sources cluster around **$22–$40 per kWh thermal**: IRENA **25–30 USD/kWh** in the 2018 column, with a 2030 column **below 15 USD/kWh** that is a target; NREL SAM default **$22.0/kWhth** including freeze protection, salt inventory, pumps, and tank foundations, dollar year unlabeled ([NREL/TP-7A40-80482](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/80482.pdf)); Annex 30 **20–40 €/kWh** at 290–565 °C. Chloride Gen3 designs are higher, about **$60/kWhth** and about **$85/kWh-t** ([NREL/TP-5700-77852](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/77852.pdf); [NREL/TP-5700-88400](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy24osti/88400.pdf)). Those figures include tanks and heat-transfer equipment for a power plant. They are not a solid-media industrial heat battery, and the electricity round trip near **44%** does not apply when the battery delivers process heat.

## Europe’s four-hour lithium-ion point already sits near the middle

The model stores BloombergNEF utility-scale 4-hour costs as **$/kW**, so **$/kWh = CAPEX / 4**. The Europe high-income peer is about **$1,011/kW in 2025 (~$253/kWh)** and **$727/kW in 2030 (~$182/kWh)**. Country rows already differ. The 2030 number is a learning forecast. Public 2024–2026 surveys show why **$253/kWh** is a bit high for 2025 Europe and why **$182/kWh** is a reasonable 2030 central case, provided pack prices are not averaged in.

Packs are not systems. BloombergNEF’s volume-weighted pack price across end uses was **$108/kWh in 2025**; **stationary-storage packs were $70/kWh**, and the lowest observed LFP stationary pack was **$50/kWh** ([BNEF, 9 Dec 2025](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/lithium-ion-battery-pack-prices-fall-to-108-per-kilowatt-hour-despite-rising-metal-prices-bloombergnef/)). The same year’s **turnkey system** survey, reported by Energy-Storage.News because the survey PDF is paywalled, has a global 4-hour average of **$110/kWh** and a global all-duration average of **$117/kWh**. Regional turnkey averages, duration mix not restated, are **China $73/kWh, Europe $177/kWh, United States $219/kWh** ([Energy-Storage.News, 16 Dec 2025](https://www.energy-storage.news/battery-storage-system-prices-continue-to-fall-sharply-bnef-and-ember-reports-find/)). IRENA, describing BNEF turnkey costs, says they are battery-provider and integrator prices and **exclude EPC, grid connection, and development** ([IRENA 2023 costs](https://www.irena.org/Publications/2024/Sep/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2023)). The 2025 footnote that would confirm the same boundary was not public, so **$177/kWh is the Europe equipment-system anchor, not a fully installed AC project**. Europe turnkey in the prior survey was about **$275/kWh in 2024**. Stationary packs at **$70/kWh** are about 64% of the global 4-hour turnkey price and are not the installed project.

Fully installed anchors sit on either side of the model. Ember, as reported in the same December 2025 article (the Ember PDF was not opened), puts all-inclusive capex for a **4-hour or longer** project, averaged across markets **excluding China and the United States** and including Italy, India, and Saudi Arabia, at **$125/kWh** as of October 2025: about **$75/kWh** core equipment and **$50/kWh** installation and connection, with connection fees from **$30 to $100/kWh**. That **$125/kWh is not a Germany/UK figure**. IRENA’s utility-scale installed cost is **$192/kWh in 2024** and **$273/kWh in 2023 USD**, a global average pulled by China ([IRENA 2024 digital report](https://www.irena.org/Digital-Report/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2024)). NREL’s US 4-hour overnight cost for a 60 MW LFP plant is **$334/kWh in 2024 dollars** (**$1,336/kW**), of which battery cabinets are **$210/kWh**; interconnection **upgrade** costs are excluded, and near-term 2025 low/mid/high costs in the same 2024 dollars are **$295 / $321 / $350** ([Cole et al., 27 June 2025](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy25osti/93281.pdf)). Lazard’s June 2025 US 100 MW / 4-hour case has an initial installed cost of **$62–160 million**, which is **$155–400/kWh** derived; the DC block alone is **$107–232/kWh** and EPC is **$28–116/kWh** ([Lazard LCOE+, June 2025](https://www.lazard.com/media/5tlbhyla/lazards-lcoeplus-june-2025-_vf.pdf)). S&P via JRC shows Germany delivery **below $200/kWh for 2025**, and the figure note says DC, so it may be a DC system price rather than a fully installed AC project ([JRC141463](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC141463/JRC141463_01.pdf)). Adding Ember’s about **$50/kWh** install-and-connection to BNEF’s Europe turnkey **$177/kWh** gives about **$227/kWh**. Putting the top of the connection range (**$100/kWh**) on that turnkey price gives about **$277/kWh** before extra European EPC. The model’s **$253/kWh** sits inside that bracket: above 2025 Europe turnkey, below a US overnight plant, and in the same neighborhood as 2024 Europe turnkey plus a modest adder.

The recommended **$150 / $230 / $330** (2025) and **$120 / $180 / $280** (2030) are judgment bands placed on those anchors for a **multi-MW, ~4-hour** battery behind a heat pump. They are not one institution’s published low/central/high. The 2025 low (**$150/kWh**) is above Ember’s ex-China, ex-US all-in and below Europe turnkey, and it fits a site with spare import capacity. The 2025 central (**$230/kWh**) is Europe turnkey plus about $50/kWh of install and connection; **$253/kWh** is a reasonable central-to-slightly-high point inside the band. The 2025 high (**$330/kWh**) is near NREL’s **$334/kWh** and below Lazard’s **$400/kWh** high. A 1 MW catalogue case at **$470/kWh in 2024 USD** (range **$350–540**) is a stale, small-unit ceiling, not the European market price ([DEA-method catalogue for Colombia](https://ens.dk/media/8617/download)). If the battery is closer to 1 MW than to tens of MW, costs move toward Lazard’s C&I DC capital of **$238–445/kWh** on a **1 MW, 2-hour** unit, which is a different product. Residential Germany at **$862/kWh in Q3 2023** is the wrong analogue ([IRENA 2023](https://www.irena.org/Publications/2024/Sep/Renewable-Power-Generation-Costs-in-2023)).

For 2030, ENTSO-E/ENTSOG TYNDP uses **€716,480/MW** for a 4-hour utility battery, which is **€179/kWh** derived, in real terms, euro price year unstated ([TYNDP 2024 methodology](https://2024.entsos-tyndp-scenarios.eu/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/TYNDP_2024_Scenarios_Methodology_Report_Final_Version_250128.pdf)). At the JRC’s 2024 rate of **1 USD = 0.9243 EUR**, that is about **$194/kWh**, close to the model’s **$182/kWh**, with the caveat that the TYNDP price year was not found. NREL’s US 2030 overnight costs in **2024$/kWh** are **$207 / $279 / $354**. BNEF’s published outlook point in the article is **2035, not 2030**: Europe 4-hour turnkey **$101/kWh**. S&P’s Germany narrative is decline through 2025 and **stabilisation by 2030**. IEA’s **−40% by 2030** on full project upfront cost runs from a 2023/early-2024 baseline; turnkey prices already fell about 40% in 2024 and 31% in 2025, so applying another 40% to 2025 Europe prices would double-count ([IEA, Batteries and Secure Energy Transitions](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/cb39c1bf-d2b3-446d-8c35-aae6b1f3a4a0/BatteriesandSecureEnergyTransitions.pdf)). China turnkey at **$73/kWh (2025)** or **$41/kWh (2035)** is an equipment floor, not the EU low case. The hardware cost does not change because the battery only buffers a heat pump; what changes is grid-connection depth, land, and developer margin, which is the Ember **~$50/kWh** bucket and which a site with spare import capacity may not pay in full.

## Conclusion

The rerun that changes LCOH is a scope test on the heat pump, not a small wiggle around **$700 and $870**. Those two numbers are a coherent equipment case; the Danish installed central case of **1,550 €/kW_th** for hot water to 150 °C sits well above **$870/kW_th** at any rate near 1.0–1.2 USD per EUR, and the 2030 catalogue barely learns that gap away. The heat battery is the opposite problem: the storage term is already at a defensible media cost, and the error that would dominate LCOH is pasting a vendor all-in **$/kWh** onto a model that still pays for charge and delivery in **$/kW**. Lithium-ion does not need a new central 2030 number. It needs a wide real-cost fan around the learning path the model already uses, with pack prices and China domestic turnkey kept out of the EU installed row.
