# Industrial heat battery CAPEX

Scope of these notes: on-site industrial process heat from resistively charged sensible thermal storage (roughly 200–1500 °C), sources dated 2018–2026. Grid-scale electricity storage and ice/chilled-water storage are out of scope except where a cited figure is easy to misread as process-heat CAPEX. Molten-salt CSP TES is kept in its own block. Numbers are quoted in the original unit and currency year when the source states one. No CPI conversion was applied here.

The user’s Energy Innovation baseline is **not** used as the sensitivity range. It is recorded only so later synthesis does not double-count it. Rissman, Energy Innovation, July 2023: input equipment $100/kW-in (wires, switches, transformers, analogized to lithium-ion balance of plant), heat-delivery equipment $300/kW-out (heat exchangers “connected to today’s boilers”), storage medium $5/kWh average with a $1.5–$10/kWh band “based on estimates from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory and thermal battery manufacturers,” round-trip efficiency 95%, capital recovery factor 14% (7% WACC over 10 years or 11% over 15 years). The $1.5–$10/kWh band cites NREL’s ENDURING particle-TES electricity-storage concept and a 18 April 2023 Q&A with Antora, so it is not an independent industrial-heat catalogue. A separate O&M line was not found in the cost-buildup section that was read. Daniel Stack is cited as having reviewed the report on 19 June 2023; the review text was not retrieved. — [Energy Innovation, Industrial Thermal Batteries (13 July 2023)](https://energyinnovation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/2023-07-13-Industrial-Thermal-Batteries-Report-v133.pdf)

## What CAPEX do catalogues and studies report for sensible-heat industrial thermal storage at roughly 200–1500 °C?

### Takeaway
Independent component costs for simple solid stores (brick, rock, sand) are a few dollars per kWh thermal when power equipment is excluded or minimal, while realized high-temperature industrial regenerators and any “total installed” heat-battery quote land in the tens of dollars per kWh. Catalogue figures that look like $0.1–$35/kWh usually mix low-temperature water tanks with high-temperature media and should not be used as a brick-battery cost.

### Cited Findings
- A 2025 Applied Energy study of firebrick resistance-heated storage for industrial process heat, modeling a design from Stack and Forsberg, reports CAPEX of $4.55/kWhth, $20.67/kW charge power (“the addition of more resistive heaters within the firebrick”), and $6.47/kW discharge power (“the addition of pumps/blowers”). The same paper’s LCOH model uses Stack et al. installed costs for firebrick, insulation brick, blowers, containment, wiring, and transformers. Charge efficiency is 0.99, discharge efficiency to air is 0.85, and radiative loss is 3% over 24 h. Currency year is not stated. Temperature capability is described as up to 1800 °C. O&M in that LCOH model is the authors’ assumption of 2.5% of normalized amortized CAPEX, not a vendor quote. Asset life used in the NPV is 30 years. — [McMillan et al. / OSTI, Applied Energy 402 (2025) 126873](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)
- The same paper says sensible-heat systems in the literature have “estimated installed costs less than $10/kWh,” with examples of rock beds producing hot air at 600 °C (basalt) and 500 °C (magnetite). That sentence is a literature characterization, not a new measurement. — [Applied Energy 2025](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)
- A Danish EUDP high-temperature rock-bed project (heated to 600 °C by an electric heater) estimates the storage system—hot rocks and capsule, excluding the heat-recovery steam generator and turbine—at 43 million DKK, stated as 2.3 €/kWh, for 2500 MWh and 62 MW thermal on a 40-hour charge/discharge cycle. The modeling text says capacity “can be utilized … at an efficiency of close to 100%.” The pilot measurement is different: best air round-trip efficiency was approximately 78%, with outlet air above 500 °C from a 600 °C heater. Recovery-system costs (power-plant retrofit, not process-heat delivery) are extra: 64.5 million DKK for a retrofit at 24% electricity efficiency, 235 million DKK for a new HRSG, 430 million DKK for a greenfield plant at 40% electricity efficiency. Estimate year is not stated. — [EUDP HT-TES final report](https://eudp.dk/files/media/document/Final%20report%20HT%20TES%20-%20the%20end.pdf)
- U.S. DOE Storage Innovations 2030 (2023) says moving-particle TES with refractory-insulated concrete silos and silica sand at about $30–$40/ton “has the potential for installed costs near $2/kWht” and an LCOS of $0.05/kWhe. This is a potential for an electricity-storage pathway, not a built industrial heat battery. — [DOE, Findings from Storage Innovations 2030: Thermal Energy Storage](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf)
- IEA ECES Annex 30 (public report, September 2018) reports realized storage-capacity cost for a steelmaking regenerator of 15–40 €/kWh at 1250–1400 °C, 140 MWh, 140 MW, cycled from twice per hour to once per three hours. A glass-production regenerator at 300–1400 °C has no cost (N/A). A steam accumulator at 150–230 °C, up to 30 MWh, is 70–300 €/kWh. Currency year of the euro figures is not stated. These are installed storage-capacity costs (investment divided by storage capacity), so short-duration regenerators fold power equipment into €/kWh. — [IEA ECES Annex 30](https://iea-es.org/wp-content/uploads/public/Applications-of-Thermal-Energy-Storag-in-the-Energy-Transition-Annex-30-Report-1.pdf)
- The same Annex 30 report’s direct two-tank molten-salt CSP row is 20–40 €/kWh at 290–565 °C, on the order of 6 GWh (Atacama example), about one cycle per day. Indirect two-tank molten salt (290–390 °C) has no cost filled in. — [IEA ECES Annex 30](https://iea-es.org/wp-content/uploads/public/Applications-of-Thermal-Energy-Storag-in-the-Energy-Transition-Annex-30-Report-1.pdf)
- A 2023 review in *Processes* reports that Laing et al. found a hypothetical concrete-module TES substituting for molten salt at Andasol-I could reach 14.1 EUR/kWht. The review also says PCM systems typically cost EUR 10–50 per kWh. Those are citations of earlier work, not 2023 bids, and the euro year is not restated. — [Enescu et al., Processes 11 (2023) 729](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9717/11/3/729)
- IRENA’s 2020 outlook, industry section, gives sensible storage (explicitly including tank TES) a 2018 cost band of 0.1–35 USD/kWh and a 2030 band of 0.1–25 USD/kWh, with the prose stating that sensible costs drop almost 30% from USD 35/kWh to USD 25/kWh. The temperature axis on that industry sensible row spans about −150 °C to 1000 °C, so the band is not a brick-battery quote. Efficiency on the industry sensible row is 50–90% (2018) and 60–90% (2030). Several solid-state industry cells are marked as values unavailable because of low technology readiness. — [IRENA, Innovation Outlook: Thermal Energy Storage (2020)](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2020/Nov/IRENA_Innovation_Outlook_TES_2020.pdf)
- IEA’s Renewables for Industry executive summary says thermal storage “built from low-cost, simple materials such as sand, cement and bricks” can store heat up to 1000 °C and costs “around USD 15–20 per kWh,” and that this is cheaper than chemical batteries. The retrieved page does not state currency year, duration, or whether heaters and heat exchangers are inside the figure. — [IEA, Renewables for Industry, executive summary](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-for-industry/executive-summary)

### Inferences
- For solid media, a storage-only or storage-plus-simple-containment cost near $2–$5 per kWh thermal is what the engineering studies actually calculate (EUDP rocks, Stack firebrick, DOE sand potential). A figure near $15–$40 per kWh thermal is what shows up when the source is either a realized industrial regenerator, a vendor module price, or an IEA system-level remark.
- IRENA’s 0.1–35 USD/kWh industry band is too wide, and too mixed with low-temperature water storage, to set a brick-battery sensitivity.

### Gaps
- Danish Energy Agency technology-catalogue Excel datasheets for electric boilers and for large thermal energy storage were not successfully read as numeric tables. The large-thermal-storage chapter that was retrieved is pit, borehole, aquifer, and tank storage for district heating (2020 price level), not 200–1500 °C solid media. A 2016 DTU paper cites the 2015 DEA catalogue for electric boilers, but the extracted table columns are scrambled, so no €/kW investment or O&M figure is reported from it here. — [DEA technology catalogues](https://ens.dk/en/analyses-and-statistics/technology-catalogues); [DTU/Orbit 2016](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/126597670/Electric_boilers_in_district_heating_systems_Lule_2016_2_.pdf)
- No catalogue entry was found that states 2025 and 2030 component costs ($/kW charge, $/kW delivery, $/kWh storage) for a commercial brick heat battery.
- Sandia-authored CSP TES cost tables were not retrieved as a standalone report. Sandia appears as a partner on particle-TES demonstration in the DOE 2023 assessment, without a separate $/kWhth quote.

## What do commercial developers claim, and what do independent reviews cite?

### Takeaway
Public vendor numbers are mostly levelized energy costs or undated module prices, not a three-part CAPEX. The one widely cited “total installed” band ($85–$210/kWh by 2025, $40–$110/kWh by 2030) is a compilation of company projections gathered by Energy Innovation and repeated by Brattle, not an independent cost buildup. Several named developers have no retrieved public CAPEX at all.

### Cited Findings
Vendor claims (label as vendor, not independent):

- Rondo has not disclosed system cost. In Canary Media, CEO John O’Donnell estimated the heat batteries can provide energy storage “well within $20 a megawatt-hour,” compared with lithium-ion battery packs at about $140/MWh (BloombergNEF, November, year of the BNEF figure as stated in the article). That is a cost of stored energy, not CAPEX per kWh of capacity. Temperature and duration are not in that sentence. — [Canary Media, Rondo heat batteries](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/clean-industry/rondos-heat-batteries-will-soon-be-cleaning-up-factories-in-us-europe)
- Antora, as author of a California Energy Commission final report (February 2024, CEC-500-2024-011), writes that thermal batteries could be deployed “for less than $10 per kilowatt-hour (kWh) of energy.” The surrounding paragraph is about long-duration electricity storage using thermophotovoltaics, not heat-only delivery to a process. The report disclaimer says it does not necessarily represent CEC views. — [CEC-500-2024-011](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2024-02/CEC-500-2024-011.pdf)
- A California grant listing for Antora states: “The installed capital costs of Antora’s thermal battery are anticipated to be <$0.05/kWh when commercialized, this is 20x lower than lithium-ion batteries.” That figure is two orders of magnitude below the <$10/kWh sentence in Antora’s own CEC report. Treat both as vendor projections and do not reconcile them into one CAPEX. — [Energize Innovation / CEC project page](https://www.energizeinnovation.fund/projects/solid-state-long-duration-energy-storage-industrial-applications)
- EnergyNest’s industry page states HEATCRETE concrete storage is “around 20–25 US dollars per kilowatt hours,” with a module up to 2 MWh thermal in a 40-foot container, and says the system is “virtually maintenance-free” so operating costs are “a fifth of what other storage systems require.” No currency year, temperature, or duration is attached to the $20–$25 figure on that page. A 2025 EnergyNest guide says the ThermalBattery becomes cost-effective at a gas price of 40 euros per MWh or more, plus CO2 charges of about 20 to 30 euros per MWh (about 90 euros per tonne), and claims an efficiency advantage of about 20% versus fossil combustion. That guide does not restate a $/kWh CAPEX. — [EnergyNest industry page](https://energy-nest.com/industry_application/); [EnergyNest 2025 industry guide](https://energy-nest.com/wp-content/uploads/energynest-leitfaden-2025-03-06-en.pdf)
- Lumenion (steel, up to 650 °C), via press officers, claimed operation at €0.02 per kWh assuming a 40-year life and 150 cycles per year, and storage “for two cents per kWh or less,” including a 2.4 MWh Vattenfall pilot. That is a levelized storage cost, not CAPEX. The company said up to 25% of stored energy can be returned as electricity and that overall efficiency is around 95% if the remaining 100–120 °C heat is used. — [pv magazine, 28 Feb 2020](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2020/02/28/storage-highlights-2020-megawatt-winner-lumenion/); [Energy-Storage.News, Vattenfall / Lumenion](https://www.energy-storage.news/vattenfall-pilots-high-temperature-steel-with-up-to-48hrs-energy-storage-duration/)
- Kyoto Group’s Heatcube is described by the company as a molten-salt thermal battery for industrial process heat at 150–300 °C, sold as heat-as-a-service or as an owned asset. No $/kWh or $/kW figure was on the retrieved product page. — [Kyoto Group](https://www.kyotogroup.no/)

Company projections compiled by analysts (not independent engineering):

- Brattle (October 2023) says Figure 5, sourced to Energy Innovation’s 30 June 2023 comments to the U.S. Treasury, shows thermal-battery total installed cost (manufacturing and installation) of about $85 to $210 per kWh for installation by 2025, $40 to $110 per kWh by 2030, and $20 to $55 per kWh by 2035. Currency year is not stated. Brattle’s own 2030 cases use a single bundled CapEx of $57.5/kWhth for a 24 MWhth store serving 1 MWth average demand, “based on communication with technology providers.” Brattle’s efficiencies in that table are resistive heating 95%, thermal storage 98%, and thermal conversion 99%. Companies named for temperature and medium, without individual prices, are Antora (carbon blocks, up to 1500 °C), Caldera (aluminum and crushed rock, primarily 90–300 °C), Brenmiller bGen (crushed rocks, 130–750 °C), Electrified Thermal Solutions Joule Hive (firebrick, up to 1800 °C), and Rondo RHB300 (brick, 80–1500 °C). — [Brattle, Thermal Batteries (2023)](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Thermal-Batteries-Opportunities-to-Accelerate-Decarbonization-of-Industrial-Heat.pdf)

Unreliable secondary (do not use in a sensitivity):

- A 2026 blog (howtostoreelectricity.com) states brick storage at 20–40 USD/kWh thermal and Antora at 30–50 USD/kWh thermal, with refractory brick at 200–1500 USD/tonne and a media-only sketch of 5–20 USD/kWh. No primary citation is given for the installed totals. These are not independent estimates.

### Inferences
- The $85–$210/kWh (2025) and $40–$110/kWh (2030) band is a duration-bundled total installed cost from developers, passed through Energy Innovation. It cannot be compared with a $5/kWh storage-medium term without knowing hours of storage and the charge and discharge ratings.
- Rondo’s “within $20/MWh” and Lumenion’s €0.02/kWh are levelized costs. They do not imply a CAPEX.
- Antora’s <$0.05/kWh and <$10/kWh statements conflict if both are read as installed capital cost. Neither should be used as a central 2025 CAPEX.

### Gaps
- No public CAPEX in $/kWh or $/kW was retrieved for Kraftblock, Electrified Thermal Solutions, Malta, 1414 Degrees, or Brenmiller. Brenmiller and ETS appear only as temperature and medium entries in the Brattle company table.
- Kyoto Group: no numeric CAPEX retrieved.
- Stack’s 19 June 2023 review of the Energy Innovation report is cited but the comments themselves were not found.
- No independent (non-vendor, non-Energy-Innovation) published invoice or EPC quote for a Rondo, Antora, or EnergyNest installation was found.

## What do IRENA, IEA, NREL, the Danish Energy Agency, Sandia, and academic reviews say for molten-salt power-tower TES versus solid-media electric thermal storage?

### Takeaway
Commercial two-tank molten-salt TES for CSP clusters around $20–$40 per kWh thermal (about 25–30 USD/kWh in IRENA’s 2018 column; $22/kWhth in NREL’s SAM 2020 default; 20–40 €/kWh in Annex 30), with thermal retention often above 90%. Chloride-salt Gen3 designs are several times higher ($60–$85/kWhth). Solid electric thermal storage is cheaper per kWh in bottom-up media models and is not the same product: its electricity-to-heat efficiency can exceed 95%, whereas molten salt paired with a steam turbine for electricity output is around 44% round trip.

### Cited Findings
Molten salt / CSP (do not mix into the brick-battery range):

- IRENA 2020, power-sector commercial molten salt (standalone): 2018 cost 25–30 USD/kWh, 2030 cost below 15 USD/kWh; efficiency above 90% (2018) and above 92% (2030); temperature below 565 °C in 2018 and 600–700 °C in 2030; lifetime 10,000 cycles in 2018 and above 10,000 in 2030. The prose says TES costs for power-generation technologies may fall by over 50% to USD 15/kWh by 2030. — [IRENA TES outlook 2020](https://www.irena.org/-/media/Files/IRENA/Agency/Publication/2020/Nov/IRENA_Innovation_Outlook_TES_2020.pdf)
- NREL (Kurup et al., February 2022, NREL/TP-7A40-80482), SAM version 2020.11.29 default: “Molten salt TES and HTF system” at $22.0/kWhth, including freeze-protection, high-temperature HTF pumps, foundations and support structures for the two tanks, and salt inventory. Dollar year is not labeled on that table. The same report cites a claim that thermal efficiency of the thermal storage and heat-transfer system at the Hami molten-salt tower “reaches 99% in operation” (Keck et al., 2019). — [NREL/TP-7A40-80482](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/80482.pdf)
- NREL Gen3 liquid-pathway preprint: chloride-salt TES, internally insulated carbon steel with refractory liner, four tanks, heat loss set so that tank loss is 2% over 24 hours, estimated at $60/kWhth, versus a Gen3 roadmap target of $15/kWhth, for a 100 MWe net plant. Dollar year is not stated in the retrieved text. — [NREL/TP-5700-77852](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/77852.pdf)
- NREL 2024 geopolymer-insulation molten-salt tank study: nitrate-salt TES about $33/kWh-t versus $26.3/kWh-t for a reference conventional metal-tank system (Worley/Advisian), designed capacity about 2791 MWh-t; chloride-salt TES about $85/kWh-t at about 2400 MWh-t because two hot and two cold tanks are required. Dollar year is not stated in the abstract. — [NREL/TP-5700-88400](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy24osti/88400.pdf)
- NREL ATB 2023: representative molten-salt power tower with 10 hours of two-tank TES, CAPEX about $7,254/kWe in 2021$ for the whole plant (solar multiple 2.4), not a TES-only cost. — [NREL ATB 2023 CSP](https://atb.nrel.gov/electricity/2023/concentrating_solar_power)
- DOE Storage Innovations 2030, Table 1, molten salt with a steam turbine, 100 MW and 10 hours, 2030 estimates taken from Viswanathan et al. (2022): round-trip efficiency 44%, storage block $88/kWhe, balance of plant $16/kWhe, power equipment $1,645.7/kWe, fixed O&M $53.7/kWe-year, variable O&M $0/kWhe, storage-block life 35 years. These are electricity-output costs. The text says molten salt “has only been deployed in a heat-to-electricity configuration.” — [DOE SI 2030 TES](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf)
- Annex 30 direct two-tank molten salt: 20–40 €/kWh, 290–565 °C (see previous section). — [IEA ECES Annex 30, September 2018](https://iea-es.org/wp-content/uploads/public/Applications-of-Thermal-Energy-Storag-in-the-Energy-Transition-Annex-30-Report-1.pdf)

Solid-media electric / process-heat storage (separate from the molten-salt cluster):

- Stack/Forsberg firebrick via Applied Energy 2025: $4.55/kWhth, $20.67/kW charge, $6.47/kW discharge; charge 99%, discharge to air 85% (see first section). — [Applied Energy 2025](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)
- EUDP rock bed: 2.3 €/kWh storage-only; measured air round trip about 78% at 600 °C. — [EUDP HT-TES](https://eudp.dk/files/media/document/Final%20report%20HT%20TES%20-%20the%20end.pdf)
- DOE sand potential near $2/kWht (electricity pathway). — [DOE SI 2030 TES](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf)
- IEA sand/cement/brick remark: around USD 15–20/kWh up to 1000 °C. — [IEA Renewables for Industry](https://www.iea.org/reports/renewables-for-industry/executive-summary)
- Brattle, describing heat stored and later used as heat: approximately 90–98% efficiency, “with maximum efficiency limited by the effectiveness of heat transfer processes and quality of insulation,” contrasted with electricity round trip that “cannot achieve greater than approximately 47%.” MIT Energy Initiative is cited for that contrast. — [Brattle 2023](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Thermal-Batteries-Opportunities-to-Accelerate-Decarbonization-of-Industrial-Heat.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible molten-salt CSP storage cost for nitrate two-tank systems, in sources from 2018–2022, is about $22–$40 per kWh thermal. The 2030 IRENA figure below $15/kWh and the Gen3 $15/kWhth target are targets, not current nitrate-plant costs. Chloride Gen3 designs at $60–$85/kWhth are a different, less mature tank technology.
- Solid electric storage and molten-salt CSP should not share one sensitivity. The solid-media energy term in bottom-up studies is several times lower than nitrate TES, and the relevant efficiency for process heat is electricity-to-heat (about 90–99% in the sources above, or about 78% in the EUDP air pilot), not the 44% electricity-to-electricity round trip.

### Gaps
- No Sandia-only cost table (for example a Turchi/Sandia two-tank buildup with an explicit dollar year) was retrieved beyond the NREL SAM default and the DOE assessment.
- Danish Energy Agency: no high-temperature solid-media datasheet numbers were extracted. The agency’s published large-storage update that was retrieved is low-temperature district-heat storage.
- IEA SHC’s June 2023 compact-TES position paper discusses industrial process heat qualitatively and does not give a $/kWh CAPEX in the retrieved text. — [IEA SHC compact TES position paper, June 2023](https://iea-shc.org/Data/Sites/1/publications/IEA_SHC_Technology_Position_Paper_Compact_Thermal_Energy_Storage_June2023.pdf)

## What low, central, and high storage and power CAPEX are defensible for 2025 and 2030?

### Takeaway
There is no single published low/central/high set in a common currency year. The defensible 2025 sensitivity is a scope-labeled range, not a statistical confidence interval. For storage energy of a solid electric thermal battery, excluding process heat exchangers, studies support a low near $2–$5/kWhth, a central that should not be set below about $5/kWhth or above the IEA’s about $15–$20/kWh without saying which scope, and a high of about $15–$40/kWhth for realized or vendor module costs. Charge and discharge power do not have an independent three-point range. For 2030, the only quantified declines are molten-salt CSP targets and company total-installed bundles, which are different objects from the 2025 component costs.

### Cited Findings
All figures below are repeated from earlier sections so the range is traceable. No new numbers are introduced.

- Storage, solid, low end of published calculations: about $2/kWht potential (DOE sand, electricity pathway); 2.3 €/kWh rock bed plus capsule (EUDP, 600 °C, 40 h, 2500 MWh); $4.55/kWhth firebrick system (Stack via Applied Energy 2025). Literature characterization “less than $10/kWh” installed. — [DOE SI 2030](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf); [EUDP](https://eudp.dk/files/media/document/Final%20report%20HT%20TES%20-%20the%20end.pdf); [Applied Energy 2025](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)

### Inferences
These bands are a reading of the citations above. They are not a published consensus, and they are not inflated to 2025 or 2030 dollars. Where a source omits the currency year, the year of the document is noted instead.

Solid-media electric thermal battery, storage energy only (media, insulation, containment; not process heat exchangers and not grid interconnection), for a 2025 sensitivity case:

- Low: about $2–$5 per kWh thermal (DOE potential and the Stack/EUDP calculations). The $2/kWht figure is a potential, not a 2025 commercial EPC price.
- Central: about $5–$15 per kWh thermal. The bottom of this band matches the Stack calculation ($4.55/kWhth, dollar year unstated, reported in a 2025 paper). The top is just under the IEA’s about USD 15–20/kWh system remark, which may already include more than media.
- High: about $15–$40 per kWh thermal if the case is meant to resemble realized regenerators (15–40 €/kWh, Annex 30, 2018) or vendor concrete modules (EnergyNest 20–25 USD/kWh, year unstated).

Do not put the company total-installed band ($85–$210/kWh in 2025, $40–$110/kWh in 2030) in the same row as $5/kWh. It is an alternative, bundled metric. Using it as “high storage CAPEX” while also adding $100/kW and $300/kW would double-count power equipment.

Charge power, 2025, solid electric battery:

- The only non-Energy-Innovation component number is about $21/kW for resistive heaters (Stack). A sensitivity that must also cover electrical interconnection equipment has no second independent point; the next published point is the excluded baseline at $100/kW-in. A span of roughly $20–$100/kW is therefore a scope span (heaters only versus heaters plus electrical balance of plant), not a low/central/high forecast. No 2030 learning rate for this term was found.

Discharge power, 2025, process heat:

- About $6.50/kW is blowers for hot air (Stack) and is the wrong scope for steam or a process heat exchanger.
- No independent low/central/high for heat-exchanger or steam-delivery CAPEX was found. Leaving delivery at the existing $300/kW-out baseline, and varying only storage energy, is more defensible than inventing a delivery range.

2030:

- Solid-media component costs: no sourced real-cost decline was found for the $4.55/kWhth or $21/kW terms. Do not apply a 2030 reduction to them without a new source.
- Company bundled total installed cost, if used as its own scenario and not added on top of component costs: $40–$110 per kWh by 2030 (company projections compiled in 2023; currency year unstated).
- Molten-salt CSP, separate scenario: IRENA’s 2030 column is below 15 USD/kWh, down from 25–30 USD/kWh in the 2018 column. NREL’s Gen3 chloride estimate remains $60/kWhth against a $15/kWhth target. Those are not brick-battery 2030 costs.

Suggested reporting currencies if a model needs one case: state the solid-media storage band as nominal USD of mixed document years (2018–2025), and state the molten-salt band separately as about $22–$40/kWhth for nitrate two-tank systems in 2018–2022 sources, with a 2030 target below $15/kWhth that is not yet the Gen3 chloride estimate.

### Gaps
- No source in this review publishes a 2025 and a 2030 low/central/high for all three of storage, charge power, and discharge power in one currency year.
- CPI inflation of the Energy Innovation baseline to 2025 and 2030 (the user’s $106/$317/$5.28 and $117/$350/$5.83) was not re-derived.
- Electric-boiler CAPEX from the Danish Energy Agency, which would bound resistive charge or heat-delivery equipment, was not extracted from the Excel datasheets.

## What is included in the quoted CAPEX?

### Takeaway
The cheap $/kWh figures are media and a simple vessel. The $20–$40/kWh molten-salt and regenerator figures include tanks, heat-transfer equipment, and often short-duration power inside the energy cost. The $85–$210/kWh company band is manufacturing plus installation for a whole battery. Almost none of the solid-media quotes clearly include a process heat exchanger sized in $/kWth.

### Cited Findings
- Energy Innovation (baseline, for comparison of scope only): three lines — electrical input equipment; heat exchangers for delivery; storage medium. Resistive-heater cost is not broken out separately from the storage medium in the paragraph that sets $5/kWh. — [Energy Innovation 2023](https://energyinnovation.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/2023-07-13-Industrial-Thermal-Batteries-Report-v133.pdf)
- Stack firebrick, as used by Applied Energy 2025: $4.55/kWhth is the storage CAPEX of that simple system; $20.67/kW is additional resistive heaters; $6.47/kW is pumps/blowers. The LCOH implementation adds insulation brick, containment, wiring, and transformers, and assumes O&M at 2.5% of amortized CAPEX. It does not describe a steam boiler heat exchanger. — [Applied Energy 2025](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3013276)
- EUDP: 2.3 €/kWh is rocks plus capsule only. HRSG, turbine, and piping are separate recovery-system costs aimed at electricity and district heat. — [EUDP HT-TES](https://eudp.dk/files/media/document/Final%20report%20HT%20TES%20-%20the%20end.pdf)
- NREL SAM molten-salt line ($22.0/kWhth): freeze protection, HTF pumps, tank foundations, salt inventory. It does not include the power cycle ($1,040/kWe in the same SAM table) or heliostats. — [NREL/TP-7A40-80482](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/80482.pdf)
- NREL Gen3 $60/kWhth: redesigned internally insulated tanks for chloride salt, not the receiver or power cycle. — [NREL Gen3 liquid pathway](https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy22osti/77852.pdf)
- Annex 30 “realized storage capacity cost” is investment cost divided by storage capacity. For a 140 MW, 140 MWh steel regenerator, one hour of discharge means power equipment is embedded in the 15–40 €/kWh. Annex 30 also defines a storage material cost ratio (material cost divided by CAPEX) but the industrial regenerator row does not publish that ratio. — [IEA ECES Annex 30](https://iea-es.org/wp-content/uploads/public/Applications-of-Thermal-Energy-Storag-in-the-Energy-Transition-Annex-30-Report-1.pdf)
- Brattle company band: “including manufacturing and installation costs.” Not itemized into media, heaters, heat exchangers, and grid connection. — [Brattle 2023](https://www.brattle.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Thermal-Batteries-Opportunities-to-Accelerate-Decarbonization-of-Industrial-Heat.pdf)
- DOE electric-pathway molten salt table itemizes storage block, balance of plant, controls, power equipment, system integration, project development, EPC, and grid integration, plus fixed O&M. That scope is a power plant, not an industrial heat delivery skid. — [DOE SI 2030](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/9_Technology%20Strategy%20Assessment%20-%20%239%20Thermal%20Energy%20Storage_508.pdf)
- Danish Energy Agency qualitative method (electric boilers and other plants): investment is EPC or overnight cost and includes equipment plus installation (engineering, civil works, buildings, on-site grid connection, commissioning). Grid-expansion costs outside the premises are excluded. The industrial electric-boiler chapter says the cost of a new medium-voltage supply cable to the factory is not included. Numeric values sit in a separate spreadsheet that was not read. — [DEA generation catalogue, April 2026 edition text](https://ens.dk/media/8614/download); [DEA industrial process heat](https://ens.dk/media/3307/download)

### Inferences
- A model that already has separate charge-power and delivery-power CAPEX should take storage CAPEX from the media-and-containment studies ($2–$15/kWhth class), not from Annex 30 regenerators or from the Brattle total-installed band.
- A model that uses one bundled $/kWh should use the company band or the IEA $15–$20/kWh remark and should not also add $100/kW and $300/kW.

### Gaps
- For Rondo, Antora, ETS, Kraftblock, Kyoto, Malta, 1414 Degrees, and Brenmiller, public pages do not itemize media versus heaters versus heat exchangers versus installation.
- Whether the IEA “USD 15–20 per kWh” includes resistive heaters and heat exchangers is not stated on the retrieved executive-summary page.
